The Bridge That Tore Itself Apart in a 19 m/s Wind (and Why "Resonance" Is the Wrong Answer)

In November 1940 the Tacoma Narrows bridge twisted itself apart in a 19 metre-per-second wind, four months after opening. Textbooks still call it resonance. It wasn't: the wind had no beat, and the vortex rhythm didn't match the bridge. The deck's shape turned its own motion into force, a feedback loop called aeroelastic flutter.

The video shows the collapse, why the resonance story fails, what flutter and negative damping are, the equation behind the critical wind speed, and how modern bridge decks are shaped and tested so it cannot happen again.
